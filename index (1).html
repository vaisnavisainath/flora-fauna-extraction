Abstract

This project uses retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) to extract the flora and fauna named in six Indian literary texts: folk tales, novels, a children’s story, retold epic tales and a book of poems. For each book, one list of flora and one list of fauna was produced and stored in Python dictionaries, giving 249 entries in total (120 flora and 129 fauna). Many entries are given with local names and scientific names, and several elements recur across books, among them the lotus, the mango, the banyan, rice, the elephant and the cobra. The lists were not checked against the passages of the books, so they are a useful first draft, and precision and recall were not measured.

1Introduction

Indian literature refers constantly to plants and animals: the tree under which a scene is set, the bird heard at dusk, the flower offered in worship. These references belong to the cultural setting of a text, and collecting them is one way of extracting Indian cultural elements from literature. This project takes flora and fauna as a first, concrete category.

The task is done with retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), in which a language model writes its answer from passages selected by a retrieval step, not from memory alone (Lewis et al., 2020). RAG suits this task because a book is long, the plants and animals are scattered through it, and every entry can in principle be traced to a passage.

The six books differ in form and in period. They range from nineteenth-century accounts of Bengali village life to a contemporary retelling of the Mahabharata, and from folk tales to lyric poetry, so together they test the method on varied material.

Research question: What flora and fauna can retrieval-augmented generation extract from Indian literary texts, and how reliable and complete are the resulting lists?

2Objectives

  1. To select six Indian literary texts of different forms.
  2. To extract the flora and the fauna named in each text using RAG.
  3. To store the results as separate lists for each book and each category.
  4. To examine the lists: their size, and the elements that recur across books.
  5. To state what must be checked before the lists are reused.
  6. To publish the lists in a public GitHub repository and present the work on a webpage hosted through GitHub Pages.

4Method

The work was carried out in five steps.

  1. Selection of texts: Six books were chosen to cover different forms (novels, folk tales, retold tales and poetry) and different periods.
  2. Separate sources: Each book was treated as its own source, so that the entries for one book come only from that book.
  3. Retrieval and generation: For each book, passages that mention plants and animals were retrieved, and a language model was asked to list the flora and the fauna named in them. Entries often include local names, varieties or scientific names in brackets.
  4. Storage: The entries were saved in two dictionaries, flora_RAG_extraction and fauna_RAG_extraction, in the file flora_fauna.py. Each dictionary maps a book to its list of entries.
  5. Examination: The lists were counted, and the elements that recur across books were collected.

      8Discussion

      8.1 What the extraction captures

      The lists are detailed where the books are. Bengal Peasant Life, an account of village life, has the longest flora list (44 entries), and it is full of crops, field plants and trees of the Bengal countryside, with local names and many scientific names. Kari the Elephant has a long fauna list (25 entries) that follows the animals of the forest. In The Golden Threshold, 13 of the 23 fauna entries are birds, which suits a book of lyric poems. The Palace of Illusions gives lotuses, jasmine and a group of trees with ritual or mythological associations, such as the shami, the parijat and the ashoka.

      8.2 Shared elements

      The elements in Table 3 point to a shared stock of plants and animals across very different authors and forms: the lotus, the mango, the banyan and rice among plants, and the elephant, the cobra and the cow or bull among animals. Their repeated presence suggests that these are central to the way Indian settings are written, although a careful reading would need to show how each one is used in its passage.

      8.3 Answer to the research question

      RAG produces long, usable lists of the plants and animals in these books, with local and scientific names that give them more value than a bare word list. The lists are not ready to use without review: they were not checked against the passages, names are not standardised, and nothing yet links an entry to its passage. A reviewer should check each entry against the text and record the passage for each one.

      10Limitations

      • The lists were not compared with a manual annotation, so precision and recall are not reported.
      • Names are not standardised. The same plant or animal appears in several forms (for example “Vata / Banyan (Ficus indica)” in one book and “Banyan tree” in others), and the counts treat each entry as given.
      • Entries are names only. They do not give the passage or the meaning of the plant or animal in the text, so they cannot yet show how the cultural elements are used.
      • Six books are a small sample of Indian literature, and a larger corpus could give different results.

      11Conclusion

      This project used RAG to extract Indian flora and fauna from six Indian literary texts and produced twelve lists with 249 entries. The lists capture the crops and trees of rural Bengal, the forest animals of Kari the Elephant, the birds of lyric poetry and the flowers and trees of the epic, and they show elements that recur across books.

      The lists are best used as a first draft. A next step is to check every entry against its passage, standardise names and record the passage for each entry, which would also allow precision and recall to be measured and extend the work to other Indian cultural elements.
